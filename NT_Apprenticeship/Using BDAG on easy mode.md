## 0. Mindset Before You Open BDAG

**BDAG is a scalpel, not a study Bible.**  
It’s not there to give you sermons; it’s there to help you:

1. See a word’s possible meanings.
    
2. Match the _right_ meaning to your _specific_ verse.
    
3. Check how that word behaves across the NT.
    

If you remember that, you’ll stay calm and focused.

---

## 1. Start With the Text (Not BDAG)

1. Read your verse in English (BSB or another good translation).
    
2. Look at the Greek text (even if you’re just recognizing shapes for now).
    
3. Identify the **Greek word you want to study**:
    
    - Example: in Mark 1:15 – _τὸ εὐαγγέλιον_.
        

Only _then_ go to BDAG. Never start in BDAG and then go hunting in the Bible.

---

## 2. Find the Headword in BDAG

1. Look up the **lexical form**, not the inflected form:
    
    - _εὐαγγέλιον_ (not _εὐαγγελίου_, _εὐαγγελίῳ_, etc.).
        
2. The BDAG entry will be laid out something like:
    
    - 1. (sense one) …
            
    - 2. (sense two) …
            
    - 3. (sense three) …
            
3. Don’t read _everything_. You’re only trying to answer one question:
    
    > “What does this word mean _here_ in my verse?”
    

---

## 3. Skim the Structure, Don’t Swallow Everything

When you open the entry:

1. **Note how many main senses** there are (1, 2, 3…).
    
2. Under each sense, you’ll see:
    
    - short English glosses (e.g., “good news,” “gospel”)
        
    - NT references (e.g., Mk 1:15; Rom 1:16; 1 Cor 15:1)
        

**Your goal:**

> Find the sub-sense where **your verse** is listed.

If Mark 1:15 is listed under sense “2,” that’s the sense you care about. Ignore the rest _for now_.

---

## 4. Lock Onto the Sense That Matches Your Verse

Once you find your verse under a particular sense:

1. **Circle/highlight** that sense number (e.g., “2.” or “II.”).
    
2. Read _just_ that mini-definition carefully.
    
3. Ask:
    
    - “Is this about general good news?”
        
    - “Is this about God’s saving message?”
        
    - “Is this about a written ‘Gospel’ book?”
        

Your verse usually fits _one_ of those clearly.

> For Mark 1:15, you’ll see it under the sense of  
> **“God’s good news / the message of the kingdom / God’s saving action.”**

That’s your working meaning.

---

## 5. Write a Simple, Context-Specific Gloss

Now translate BDAG’s dense wording into a **simple phrase** for _your_ passage.

For example:

- BDAG might give something like:  
    “God’s good news, proclaimed by Jesus and the apostles, concerning the kingdom and salvation.”
    
- You simplify for Mark 1:15:
    
    > “The announced good news that God’s kingdom has arrived.”
    

Write that in your notes right next to the Greek word. That’s your **contextual gloss**.

---

## 6. Check 2–3 Other Verses in the Same Sense

To avoid tunnel vision:

1. Look at 2–3 other verses BDAG lists under the _same sense_ as your verse.
    
2. Read them briefly in English.
    
3. Ask:
    
    - “Is the word being used in a similar way?”
        
    - “Does my gloss still make sense there?”
        

If yes → you’re on the right track.  
If no → you may need to tweak your wording.

This step teaches you that **words have families of uses**, not one magical meaning.

---

## 7. Stop Before You Drown in Data

**Do NOT** in your stage:

- Read every citation.
    
- Chase every LXX reference.
    
- Try to master the entire entry at once.
    

A good first-pass goal:

- ✅ Identify the correct sense for _your_ verse
    
- ✅ Rewrite a clear gloss in your own words
    
- ✅ Glance at 2–3 related verses
    
- ❌ Skip everything not related to your text (for now)
    

You can always come back later in Phase 1–2 when your Greek is stronger.

---

## 8. Plug BDAG Back Into Your Exegesis

Now return to your passage (e.g., Mark 1:14–15) and ask:

> “Given what _euangelion_ means here,  
> how does this clarify what Jesus is proclaiming?”

For your exegetical notes, you might write something like:

> “In Mark 1:15, _euangelion_ is not generic ‘good news’ but the specific announcement that God’s long-promised reign is arriving in and through Jesus.”

You’ve now used BDAG **correctly**:

- you didn’t hunt for theology
    
- you didn’t chase etymology
    
- you did confirm meaning from context + lexicon
    

---

## 9. A Simple “BDAG Workflow” You Can Reuse

You can literally keep this next bit as a checklist:

1. **Text first** – read the verse, identify the word.
    
2. **Look up the lexical form** in BDAG.
    
3. **Find your verse** in the entry.
    
4. **Note the sense number** and mini-definition.
    
5. **Write a simple gloss** in your own words.
    
6. **Check 2–3 other verses** in the same sense.
    
7. **Return to the passage** and update your interpretation.
    
8. **Stop there** unless you have time/energy to go deeper.
    

Do this repeatedly and BDAG will slowly become familiar instead of scary.
