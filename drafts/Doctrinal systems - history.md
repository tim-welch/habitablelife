## **1. Early Church (1st–5th centuries)**

### **Core Issues: Christology, the Trinity, and Grace**

- **Apostolic Christianity** – The New Testament era and immediate successors; doctrines not yet systematized.
    
- **Nicene / Chalcedonian Orthodoxy** – Defined the Trinity (Council of Nicaea, 325) and Christ’s dual nature (Council of Chalcedon, 451).
    
- **Augustinianism** – Augustine of Hippo’s teaching on original sin and grace; set the stage for later debates on free will.
    
- **Pelagianism** – Taught human beings can choose good without divine grace (condemned as heresy).
    
- **Semi-Pelagianism** – Tried to mediate Augustine and Pelagius, claiming humans initiate faith and grace completes it (later rejected).
    

---

## **2. Medieval Period (6th–15th centuries)**

### **Core Issues: Sacraments, Authority, and Scholasticism**

- **Roman Catholicism (as systematized)** – Developed a full sacramental and hierarchical system centered on the papacy and apostolic succession.
    
- **Eastern Orthodoxy** – Emphasized theosis (union with God through grace) and maintained continuity with the early Church Fathers.
    
- **Thomism** – Thomas Aquinas’s synthesis of faith and reason; established the intellectual framework for Catholic theology.
    
- **Mystical Traditions** – Contemplative theology (e.g., Bernard of Clairvaux, Catherine of Siena) stressing union with God experientially.
    

---

## **3. Reformation and Counter-Reformation (16th–17th centuries)**

### **Core Issues: Authority, Justification, and Church Reform**

- **Lutheranism** – Founded by Martin Luther; emphasizes justification by faith alone and the authority of Scripture.
    
- **Reformed Theology / Calvinism** – John Calvin’s system emphasizing God’s sovereignty, predestination, and covenantal structure.
    
- **Anglicanism** – Middle way between Catholicism and Protestantism; maintains liturgy and episcopacy with Reformation doctrine.
    
- **Anabaptism** – Radical Reformation movement advocating believer’s baptism, nonviolence, and separation from state power.
    
- **Arminianism** – Jacob Arminius’s response to strict Calvinism, affirming human freedom and conditional election.
    
- **Council of Trent (Catholic Counter-Reformation)** – Clarified and defended Catholic doctrines (Scripture + Tradition, seven sacraments).
    

---

## **4. Enlightenment and Post-Reformation (17th–19th centuries)**

### **Core Issues: Reason, Human Will, and Social Application**

- **Wesleyanism / Methodism** – John Wesley’s development of Arminianism, focusing on sanctification and practical holiness.
    
- **Molinism** – Luis de Molina’s “middle knowledge” solution reconciling divine sovereignty with free will.
    
- **Covenant Theology** – Protestant Reformed framework interpreting Scripture through divine covenants.
    
- **Dispensationalism** – 19th-century Protestant view dividing history into “dispensations” and distinguishing Israel from the Church.
    
- **Postmillennialism** – Optimistic eschatology expecting the world to be evangelized before Christ’s return.
    
- **Amillennialism** – Interprets the millennium symbolically as Christ’s current reign through the Church.
    

---

## **5. Modern and Contemporary (20th century–present)**

### **Core Issues: Experience, Justice, and the Nature of God**

- **Pentecostalism / Charismatic Theology** – Emphasizes the baptism of the Holy Spirit, gifts, and experiential faith.
    
- **Neo-Orthodoxy (Barth, Brunner)** – Reaction against liberalism; reasserts God’s transcendence and revelation in Christ.
    
- **Liberation Theology** – Focuses on salvation as both spiritual and social liberation from oppression.
    
- **Process Theology** – Views God as dynamically changing in relationship with creation.
    
- **Open Theism** – Asserts that God knows all possibilities but allows for genuine human freedom in an open future.
    
- **Universalism (Modern Forms)** – Reemerges with thinkers like Karl Barth and C.S. Lewis (implicitly), emphasizing eventual reconciliation of all.
